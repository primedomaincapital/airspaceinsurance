The thesis

A name that states the risk and the product.

Aviation insurance is a small market by premium and a large market by consequence. A single wide-body hull is worth hundreds of millions of dollars, a single liability limit can run past a billion, and a single political event can strand four hundred aircraft at once. The underwriters who price that risk do not need a clever brand. They need a name a broker, an airline risk manager, or a drone operator can trust at first sight.

That is what this name does. It is not a metaphor and it is not a play on words. Airspace is where the risk lives. Insurance is what is being sold. Put together, they describe the category exactly — commercial aviation, general aviation, drones and unmanned systems, aerospace and defence — without narrowing to any one of them.

The name is short, pronounceable, and exact. It works on an underwriting firm's letterhead, on a drone-insurance quote page, on a broker's placement slip, and in front of an aircraft owner who needs to know, in one glance, that this is an aviation insurance business and nothing else. It is an insurance name first, and an aviation name second — and in that order lies its value.

Specific, and broad enough to travel. A strong airspace-insurance brand must cover three linked markets at once: the airlines and lessors who buy hull and liability cover, the general-aviation owners and operators who insure smaller fleets, and the drone and unmanned operators whose policies are being written almost from scratch. This name covers all three without vagueness that says nothing at all.
