Aviation insurance, drones, and the fleet, by the numbers

Aviation insurance market size, drone insurance growth, the general-aviation fleet, airline traffic, aircraft deliveries, and the Russia lessor claims, from published sources.

Three different kinds of number sit behind this name, and they must not be mixed. The first kind measures the premium pool: the size of the aviation and drone insurance markets, counted by research firms with different scopes. The second kind measures the insured assets: the aircraft in the sky, the drones registered, and the passengers carried. The third kind measures the loss that repriced the market: the aircraft stranded in Russia and the court judgment that followed. The first kind tells a reader how much premium is at stake. The second tells a reader how many assets generate it. The third tells a reader why the price of cover moved.

What the latest numbers show is a market growing on every axis at once: the premium pool is projected to nearly double by 2035, the drone segment is growing about twice as fast as traditional aviation cover, and the fleet delivering into service is larger and more valuable than the one it replaces — while the Russia loss still sets the terms on war-risk cover.

How to read these numbers

Included: published aviation and drone insurance market sizes and forecasts, with the publisher's scope stated wherever estimates differ; FAA general-aviation and drone registration figures; IATA passenger traffic; Airbus and Boeing delivery and backlog figures as reported in early 2026; and the Russia lessor claims as decided by the English High Court in June 2025. Excluded: any single market-size headline presented without its scope or publisher; undated practitioner claims presented as dated figures; and any blending of hull, liability, and reinsurance definitions, which describe different things.

Published market-size estimates differ by scope — some count hull and liability only, others include war risk, cargo, and reinsurance — so the range is shown wherever publishers disagree, and no single figure is presented as the only measure. Forecasts are labeled as forecasts, not actuals. Registration counts are cumulative tallies; the FAA distinguishes them from the effective active fleet, and that distinction is noted in article 2.
