What Drone Insurance Costs in 2026: Hull, Liability, and the Line Items Brokers Skip

A $1 million liability policy costs about $500 a year. The bill that surprises drone operators is everything after that sentence.

Prime Domain Capital Research · November 19, 2026 · 8 min read

A Part 107 pilot in Austin opened her renewal email this spring and found two numbers that did not seem to belong to the same policy. The liability line — $1 million of cover for the photography drone she flies over rooftops — cost $612 for the year. The hull line, for the aircraft itself and the camera hanging under it, cost $2,900. She had bought the drone for $14,000. She was, in effect, paying a fifth of its value every year to insure it, and the renewal letter offered no explanation beyond the premium. She called her broker. The broker's answer was that the liability price was the market, the hull price was the aircraft, and the gap between them was the whole story of drone insurance in 2026.

Start with the line almost every commercial operator buys first, because almost every client demands it. A $1 million liability policy for a Part 107 operator runs roughly $500 to $650 a year as of 2025–2026, according to The Drone U's 2026 insurance guide, with the same range repeated across broker rate cards compiled by Daylongs and DroneBundle. The Federal Aviation Administration does not require drone operators to carry liability insurance at all. Clients do. Film permits, construction sites, government contracts, and real-estate brokerages ask for proof of $1 million in cover before the aircraft leaves the case, and The Drone U's guide puts it plainly: most clients expect that certificate before hiring. The price scales in a way that surprises first-time buyers. A $500,000 limit runs about $350 to $450 a year; doubling the limit to $2 million lifts the premium only to roughly $700 to $900, per Daylongs' 2025–2026 rate table. DroneBundle's 2026 guide puts the step from $1 million to $2 million at a 30 to 50 percent increase, not a doubling. Liability, in other words, is the cheap part of the policy, and buying more of it is the best value in the drone insurance market.

Hull is where the bill grows teeth. Hull cover — repair or replacement of the operator's own aircraft after a crash, collision, or theft — is priced as a percentage of the insured value, typically 8 to 12 percent a year, according to The Drone U and DroneBundle. Daylongs' range runs a little wider, 5 to 12 percent, with the rate moving on pilot experience and claims history. The arithmetic is unforgiving on the aircraft that dominate commercial work. A $30,000 industrial mapping drone costs $1,500 to $3,600 a year to insure for hull alone on Daylongs' figures. A DJI Matrice 350 RTK, the workhorse of inspection and mapping, sells for about $10,519 to $11,500 for the aircraft and controller, with fuller kits near $14,100, according to DroneTrader's October 2026 dealer survey — and that is before the payload. A Zenmuse P1 camera or a LiDAR unit can double the insured value, and Extreme Aerial Productions, a commercial operator in the Southwest, reports hull and liability together at $3,500 to $5,500 a year for a $45,000 survey rig. The pilot in Austin was not being overcharged. She was insuring a $14,000 aircraft at roughly the market rate, and the market rate for hull is brutal because the loss frequency is real: these aircraft crash, and when they do, the repair bill lands close to the replacement cost.

The price changes again by use case, and this is where generic rate cards mislead. Photography and real-estate work sits at the bottom of the market: a single light aircraft, a $1 million liability policy, and often no hull cover at all, for $500 to $650 a year. Inspection work — bridges, flare stacks, rooftops, power lines — sits in the middle and at the top simultaneously, because the aircraft is expensive and the operating environment is hostile. A Matrice 350 RTK with a thermal payload over an industrial site is a $20,000-plus insured value flying within meters of steel, and the premium follows the value and the site. Agriculture is the most expensive per aircraft in the market. A DJI Agras T50 spray drone sells for $17,999 to $31,500 and the larger Agras T100 for $21,818 to $39,999, per DroneTrader's 2026 pricing, and PolicyCosts' 2026 underwriting guide for agricultural spray fleets puts annual premiums at $8,000 to $18,000 per aircraft for a three-to-five-aircraft fleet. The spray drone carries chemicals, flies low over crops and crews, and needs a chemical-exposure endorsement that a photography policy never touches. The same $1 million liability limit costs a different amount in each of these three worlds, because underwriters are not pricing a limit. They are pricing what the aircraft does, where it does it, and what it carries.

Then come the line items brokers skip in the first conversation, and these are the ones that decide whether a claim pays. An on-demand policy — $5 to $15 an hour through providers such as SkyWatch.AI and AirModo, per The Drone U and DroneBundle — looks cheap until the flight hours add up. At $10 an hour, the annual $500-to-$650 liability policy wins once an operator flies more than about 50 to 65 hours a year, and a working commercial pilot clears that in a month. Ground equipment is a second gap: controllers, batteries, charging stations, and spare payloads are often excluded unless scheduled separately, and a set of Matrice TB65 batteries alone runs about $530 each, according to Extreme Aerial Productions. Non-owned and hired-aircraft cover matters for operators who rent or borrow a second airframe for a big job. Invasion-of-privacy and cyber endorsements matter for any operator whose camera records people or whose flight logs live in the cloud. And beyond-visual-line-of-sight work, the capability every serious operator wants next, is still priced as an exception: the FAA's Part 108 rule for beyond-visual-line-of-sight flight sat in White House review from July 10, 2026 and was not yet in effect in the fourth quarter of 2026, according to Air Pixels' industry benchmark report, so BVLOS flights still need a Part 107 waiver or exemption, and a policy that does not name that waiver does not cover that flight.

The counterargument is that most operators buy too much insurance, not too little, and there is evidence for it. The FAA counted 424,516 active Part 107 aircraft at the end of 2025 and had issued 493,396 Remote Pilot certificates, according to the FAA Aerospace Forecast for fiscal years 2026 to 2046 — a fleet in which the median aircraft is a sub-$2,000 quadcopter, not a $30,000 survey rig. For that pilot, hull cover at 8 to 12 percent of value is a bad trade: the premium over three years approaches the cost of a replacement aircraft, and a careful operator with a light aircraft in open country may rationally self-insure the hull and buy liability only. Brokers who sell hull to every client are selling against the arithmetic. The honest version of the argument cuts the other way too. The operator who skips hull on a $30,000 mapping rig is one hard landing from a bill that wipes out a quarter's revenue, and the operator who skips liability entirely is one gust of wind from a lawsuit. The decision is not whether insurance is worth it in general. It is which line, on which aircraft, at which value.

The way to read a drone quote in 2026 is to separate the three numbers and interrogate each one. Liability first: $1 million for about $500 to $650, and $2 million for not much more — buy the higher limit, because the marginal cost is small and the client requirements only move upward. Hull second: 8 to 12 percent of the insured value, which means the hull decision is really an aircraft-buying decision — every $10,000 of payload adds $800 to $1,200 a year forever, so the sensor choice is also a premium choice. Endorsements third: the hourly policy, the ground equipment, the privacy and cyber cover, and the waiver language for the flights the operator actually plans to fly. The pilot in Austin kept her $612 liability line, raised her hull deductible, and dropped the on-demand top-up she had never used. Her renewal fell by a fifth. Nothing about her flying changed. What changed was that she finally read the policy as three separate purchases, which is what it has always been.
