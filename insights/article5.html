Aerospace Insurance Market 2026: A $5.84 Billion Pool, Two Growth Engines, and One Loss That Rewrote the Rules

Aviation insurance is a $5.84 billion premium pool growing about 5 percent a year — until you count drones and the low-altitude economy, where growth runs twice as fast. The loss that repriced it came from a government resolution, not a crash.

Prime Domain Capital Research · November 15, 2026 · 8 min read

Early in 2026, a broker in London laid out the renewal for a mid-size aircraft lessor — hull, liability, war risk — and the war-risk line cost more than the hull line for the first time anyone at the table could remember. The aircraft had not changed. The routes had not changed. What had changed was a date: March 10, 2022, the day a Russian government resolution made it illegal to return more than 400 leased aircraft to their owners. No metal was bent. No one was hurt. And the aviation insurance market has been repricing itself around that fact ever since.

Start with the size of the pool, because it is smaller than the stakes suggest. The global aviation insurance market measured $5.84 billion in 2025 on Reports and Data's count, $5.80 billion on Emergen Research's, and $4.70 billion on Atlas Magazine's. The range is not sloppy reporting; it is scope. Some counts cover hull and liability only, others add war risk, cargo, and reinsurance. Treat the $5.8 billion figure as the working center and the spread as a warning about definitions. The forecasts agree more than the baselines do: Reports and Data projects $9.67 billion by 2035 and Emergen $9.70 billion, a compound annual growth rate of 5.2 to 5.3 percent. Hull is the largest coverage segment at 42.5 percent of the aircraft insurance market, and war and terrorism cover is the fastest-growing line from 2026 to 2035. Atlas puts the trajectory in perspective: its $3.76 billion measure for 2021 rose about 25 percent to 2025. This is a market nearly doubling in a decade, from a base most underwriters would call small.

The first growth engine is the one the traditional market barely counted five years ago: drones. The global drone insurance market reached $1.75 billion in 2024 on SkyQuest's count — $1.9 billion in 2025 on the same scope — and is forecast to reach $3.82 billion by 2033, a compound annual growth rate of 9.1 percent, roughly double the rate of traditional aviation cover. The wider low-altitude economy tells the same story faster: $1.2 billion of insurance premium in 2024, forecast to reach $3.5 billion by 2034 at 11.5 percent compound growth, per Reports and Data. The asset base behind those premiums is already enormous. The United States had 785,827 drones registered in August 2024 — 390,027 commercial — with 388,838 recreational pilots registered and more than one million drones by December 2024, according to FAA data. The FAA issued 427,598 remote-pilot certificates in 2024, up from 368,633 in 2023, and forecasts 628,600 remote pilots by 2030. Every one of those operators is a policy that does not exist yet, or a policy priced on almost no loss history. That is the drone market's opportunity and its underwriting problem in the same sentence.

The second growth engine is the fleet itself — older, busier, and more valuable than the one it replaces. The United States had 213,756 active general-aviation and Part 135 aircraft in 2024, flying more than 29 million hours. The piston fleet alone stood at 138,960 aircraft, with nearly 30,000 of them aged 45 to 49 years. General aviation contributes $339.2 billion to the American economy and supports 1.3 million jobs, according to the PwC study for the Alliance for Aviation Across America. Above that, commercial traffic keeps setting records: 4.779 billion segment passengers flew in 2024, generating 9,037 billion revenue passenger kilometres, with IATA forecasting 4.988 billion passengers in 2025 after 10.6 percent growth in 2024. The manufacturers are delivering into that demand — Airbus delivered 793 aircraft in 2025 and Boeing 600, Boeing's best annual total since 2018 — and the order backlog is now the underwriter's long horizon: Airbus holds 8,754 aircraft on order and Boeing more than 6,100, a combined backlog near 15,000 aircraft, roughly a decade of production at current rates. Insured values rise with every delivery. The premium pool grows because the metal does.

Then there is the loss that reset the market's sense of what it is actually insuring. More than 400 leased aircraft and related equipment were stranded in Russia, with an initial estimated value of up to $12 billion. The legal fight took more than three years to reach a judgment. On June 11, 2025, Mr Justice Butcher of the English High Court ruled in AerCap Ireland Ltd v AIG Europe SA & Ors, the Russian Aircraft Lessor Policy Claims case, after a trial that ran ten to twelve weeks. The claims before the court covered 147 aircraft and 16 standalone engines leased to 18 Russian airlines, including Aeroflot, with a combined insured value of more than $4.5 billion. The claimants — AerCap, DAE Capital, Merx Aviation Finance, Falcon 2019-1 Aircraft 3 Limited, and Genesis Leasing — won. The judge held that the aircraft were lost on March 10, 2022, the day Russian Government Resolution 311 banned the export of aircraft and equipment, and that the loss fell under the war-risk cover's government-perils section, as a restraint or detention by a government. It was not, the court held, an all-risks loss. The distinction mattered in dollars, not just in doctrine. AerCap, the largest claimant with 116 aircraft and 15 engines in the case, had argued the loss was an all-risks one — because its war-risk cover carried a $1.2 billion aggregate limit against a total loss of about $2.1 billion. The court disagreed, and the war-risk limit stood. Settlements reached during the proceedings had already reduced the claims in the judgment to around $3 billion. For comparison, total aviation liability losses after the September 11 attacks were estimated at about $2 billion. A single political event produced a loss roughly six times that size without a single hull being damaged.

Here is the counterargument, and it is worth taking seriously. The market's smallness is not a weakness; it is evidence that commercial aviation is extraordinarily safe, and premium growth from here comes from new exposures — drones, the low-altitude economy, air taxis — rather than from more crashes. On that reading, the Russia loss was a concentration event, not a frequency event: hundreds of aircraft, owned by a handful of lessors, leased to airlines in a single country, all exposed to one political decision at the same moment. It reprices war risk and lessor concentration. It does not necessarily reprice hull and liability, where the loss record remains the industry's best advertisement. The skeptics have a point. But the skeptics' point has a limit. Concentration is not an accident in aviation insurance; it is the structure. A handful of lessors own a large share of the world's fleet, a handful of manufacturers build it, and a handful of hubs route it. The next political shock will find the same concentrations, in different places, with different wording on the policy.

The implication for anyone pricing this risk is to watch three things, in order. First, war-risk pricing and the sub-limits lessors accept on it: the $1.2 billion aggregate that capped AerCap's recovery is now the number every lessor's broker is renegotiating. Second, the drone and low-altitude lines, where premium is growing at 9 to 11 percent a year and the loss data is still being written — the underwriter who builds the rating model first wins the segment. Third, the fleet renewal cycle: a decade of backlog means insured values keep rising even if rates do not, and an aging piston fleet flying 16 million hours a year is a different risk than a new one. The aerospace insurance market is small, growing, and — since March 2022 — priced on politics as much as on metal. The next loss will probably not come from the sky. It will come from a resolution, a sanction, or a border closing. Underwriters who price for weather and forget about governments will keep learning that lesson at the worst possible price.
