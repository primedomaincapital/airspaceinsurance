The Air Taxi Insurance Gap, Part Two: Who Insures the Passenger

The aircraft is insured. The operator is insured. The passenger, boarding a six-rotor aircraft with no loss history and a regulatory minimum written for airplanes, is protected by a patchwork nobody has tested.

Prime Domain Capital Research · November 19, 2026 · 8 min read

The passenger boards at a vertiport in Dubai, where Joby Aviation has a six-year agreement with the Roads and Transport Authority to run the world’s first commercial air taxi service. The aircraft is a Joby S4: five seats, six rotors, a range of more than a hundred miles, a cruise speed near two hundred miles an hour. The flight is twelve minutes. The passenger has no idea what insurance covers her, and no way to find out. The operator’s policy is not posted at the gate. The manufacturer’s product liability is not printed on the ticket. Her own travel insurance, if she has any, almost certainly excludes the aircraft she is about to board. She is, in the precise legal sense, a passenger in a vehicle class that has no loss history, no settled case law, and a regulatory insurance minimum written for a different kind of aircraft entirely.

The first part of this series looked at who insures the aircraft. This one looks at who insures the passenger, and the answer is a patchwork that nobody has tested. The federal minimum for a commercial air taxi operator comes from 14 CFR 205.5, the rule that sets the insurance floor for air carriers operating under Part 135. For an aircraft with sixty seats or fewer, the minimum is $75,000 per person, $300,000 per occurrence, and $100,000 for property damage, plus $75,000 per passenger multiplied by 75 percent of the number of seats. For a five-seat Joby, that arithmetic produces a passenger liability floor of roughly $281,250 per passenger. For aircraft with more than sixty seats, the minimum rises to $300,000 per person and $20 million per aircraft. These numbers were written for airplanes, and they have not kept pace with the value of a human life in an American courtroom, where a single wrongful death claim routinely exceeds $10 million.

What operators actually carry is far above the floor, and the gap between the floor and the practice is the first thing to understand. Charter operators in the United States typically carry $5 million to $50 million in smooth liability limits, meaning a single limit that covers bodily injury and property damage together, with no per-passenger sublimit. The air taxi developers have gone further. Apollo Insurance and Moonrock, the specialist aviation broker, placed a facility for the sector with $100 million in liability and $22.5 million in property coverage. Allianz, one of the world’s largest aviation insurers, has underwritten test-flight cover for the new aircraft. The market is not ignoring the risk. It is pricing it on analogy, because there is no loss history to price it on, and the analogy it uses is a mix of helicopters, light aircraft, and drones, three risk classes that have almost nothing in common with a six-rotor electric aircraft carrying fare-paying passengers over a city.

The second layer is the manufacturer. When an air taxi crashes, the first question after the rescue is whether the aircraft failed or the operator failed, and the answer decides which policy pays. The manufacturer’s product liability covers a defect in design or manufacture. The operator’s liability covers pilot error, maintenance, and dispatch. The two policies meet at the scene of the accident, and the passenger’s family waits while the insurers argue about which side of the line the cause falls on. The manufacturers know this, which is why the certification process matters as much to the insurers as it does to the regulators. A type certificate from the Federal Aviation Administration is not just permission to fly. It is the document that tells an underwriter the aircraft has been tested to a standard the underwriter understands.

The third layer is the passenger’s own cover, and it is the thinnest of the three. Most travel insurance policies exclude aircraft that are not operated by a scheduled airline, and an air taxi is not a scheduled airline. Most life insurance policies have aviation exclusions that were written for private flying and have never been tested against a commercial eVTOL flight. A passenger who is injured in an air taxi accident and looks to her own policies will very likely find that the exclusions were written before the aircraft existed, and that the insurer’s position is that the exclusion applies. The passenger’s real protection is the operator’s policy and the manufacturer’s policy, and neither is visible to her before she boards.

The regulatory picture is moving, but slowly. The Federal Aviation Administration launched its electric vertical takeoff and landing Integration Pilot Program in March 2026, a three-year data collection effort across twenty-six states, designed to gather the operational evidence that will eventually support certification standards and insurance pricing. The program is the right idea, and it is also an admission: the data the insurers need does not exist yet, and the first commercial passengers are flying while it is being collected. Dubai’s six-year agreement with Joby, and the similar arrangements being negotiated in other cities, mean the first real loss history will be written outside the United States, under legal systems and insurance regimes that American underwriters do not yet know how to read.

The counterargument deserves a hearing, because it is the argument the industry makes, and it is not frivolous. Commercial aviation is the safest form of transport ever devised, and it achieved that record under a liability regime that looks very much like the one the air taxis are entering. The operators are serious companies with serious balance sheets. Joby, Archer, and their peers have raised billions, and they have every incentive to over-insure rather than under-insure, because a single under-insured accident would end the industry before it begins. The insurers are not naive. They have priced new risk classes before, from the first jet airliners to the first commercial drones, and they have done it by starting conservative and adjusting as the data arrives. The passenger is not unprotected. She is protected by a market that is watching very closely.

The counterargument fails on one point, and it is the point that matters to the passenger. The market is watching, but the passenger is not part of the market. She cannot see the limits, she cannot compare them, and she cannot choose a better-insured operator, because the information is not published and the choice is not offered. In commercial aviation, the passenger’s protection is a matter of public record: the airline’s insurance, the international conventions, the Warsaw and Montreal limits, all of it known and settled. In air taxis, the passenger’s protection is a private contract between the operator and the insurer, and the passenger is not a party to it. That is the gap. It is not that the coverage is inadequate. It is that the passenger cannot verify it, and the system gives her no way to.

The gap closes the way insurance gaps always close: with loss history, with litigation, and with regulation. The first accident will produce the first case law, and the first case law will tell the insurers what a passenger’s life is worth in this aircraft class, which will tell the operators what limits to buy, which will tell the regulators what minimums to set. Until then, the practical advice is simple and unsatisfying. Ask the operator what limits it carries before you board, and do not be surprised when nobody at the vertiport can answer. Read your own travel and life policies for aviation exclusions before you fly, not after. And understand that the twelve-minute flight across a city is the most carefully watched twelve minutes in the history of aviation, and the least transparently insured. The aircraft is ready. The insurance is a work in progress, and the passenger is the one carrying the part of the risk that has not been priced yet.

Sources: Federal Aviation Administration, 14 CFR 205.5 (Part 135 insurance minimums: $75,000 per person, $300,000 per occurrence, $100,000 property; $75,000 per passenger x 75% of seats; over 60 seats $300,000 per person, $20 million per aircraft); Apollo Insurance / Moonrock facility ($100 million liability, $22.5 million property); Allianz test-flight cover; Federal Aviation Administration, eVTOL Integration Pilot Program (March 2026, 26 states, three-year data collection); Joby Aviation, S4 specifications (five seats, six rotors, 100+ miles, ~200 mph) and Dubai Roads and Transport Authority six-year agreement; charter-market liability practice ($5 million–$50 million smooth).
