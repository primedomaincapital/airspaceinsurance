A Million Drones, and Almost No One Insuring Them Properly

More than a million drones are registered in the United States. The insurance built for them is still catching up.

Prime Domain Capital Research · November 6, 2026 · 7 min read

A roofing contractor in Ohio bought a drone in 2023 to photograph roofs. He registered it, passed the FAA's remote-pilot test, and started charging $250 a job for aerial images. On his eleventh job, a gust pushed the aircraft into a chimney. The drone was worth $1,800. The chimney repair was $4,600. The homeowner's lawyer asked a question the contractor had never considered: who pays when a flying camera damages a house? His answer — a shrug, and a general-liability policy that had never mentioned aircraft — is the story of drone insurance in one small scene.

Multiply that scene by a million. As of August 8, 2024, the FAA counted 785,827 drones registered in the United States: 390,027 commercial drones and 388,838 recreational pilots registered, with the remainder paper registrations. By December 2024, a joint statement from the Department of Homeland Security, the FBI, the FAA, and the Department of Defense put the figure at more than one million drones lawfully registered. The FAA had issued 427,598 remote-pilot certificates in 2024, up from 368,633 in 2023 — an increase of nearly 59,000 in a single year. The FAA's Aerospace Forecast for fiscal years 2026 to 2046 projects 628,600 remote pilots by 2030, and a commercial registry of 540,845 active aircraft by the same year.

The insurance market built for those operators is growing fast, and from a small base. SkyQuest put the global drone insurance market at $1.75 billion in 2024, rising to $1.9 billion in 2025 and $3.82 billion by 2033, a compound annual rate of 9.1%. Other published estimates differ on the exact size — one puts the 2024 market at $1.5 billion in 2023 rising to $3.5 billion by 2033, another at $3.95 billion in 2024 — because they draw the boundary differently: hull alone, liability alone, or both, and commercial alone or commercial plus recreational. The direction is not in dispute. Every estimate points the same way, and the growth rate is roughly double the growth rate of traditional aviation insurance.

What is being insured is changing faster than the policies. The first generation of drone cover was hull insurance for the aircraft itself: a $1,500 quadcopter, a $12,000 survey drone. The second generation is liability: the chimney, the car roof, the wedding guest, the power line. The third generation, now being written, is operational: delivery fleets, beyond-visual-line-of-sight inspection, agricultural spraying, public-safety flights, and the low-altitude economy — a segment Reports and Data valued at $1.2 billion in 2024, projected to reach $3.5 billion by 2034 at an 11.5% annual rate. Each generation needs different wording, different limits, and different underwriting data, and most of that data does not yet exist in the form underwriters prefer: loss history, by use case, by airframe, by pilot experience.

That data gap is the market's real constraint. Traditional aviation insurance rests on a century of accident records, maintained by the FAA and the National Transportation Safety Board, with every hull loss investigated and published. Drone losses are different: they are small, frequent, often unreported, and scattered across thousands of operators who may not even know they need cover. A Part 107 certificate requires a knowledge test and recurrent training every 24 months, and flights are capped at 400 feet above ground level and 100 miles per hour, with one remote pilot operating one aircraft at a time. Those rules create a safety framework. They do not create a loss database. Underwriters pricing drone fleets today are, in effect, pricing a risk they can see growing but cannot yet measure precisely.

The established aviation insurers have noticed. Allianz, AIG, AXA XL, Munich Re, and Swiss Re all appear in published lists of drone-insurance market participants, alongside specialists such as Global Aerospace. Their interest is not sentimental. A drone fleet policy is a recurring, data-rich product: every flight can be logged, every pilot's hours counted, every airframe tracked. That is the kind of risk an insurer can price better over time, and the kind a platform can sell at scale — on-demand cover by the hour, annual fleet policies, and embedded insurance at the point of drone sale or rental.

The counterargument is that the growth story is overstated. Registration counts are cumulative tallies, not an active fleet: the FAA itself distinguishes cumulative registrations from an effective active fleet, and research published in 2026 put the effective commercial fleet at roughly 388,000 to 395,000 aircraft against nearly a million cumulative commercial registrations. New commercial registrations peaked, by the FAA's own reading, and the recreational side is shrinking in new sign-ups. If the fleet is smaller than the headlines suggest, the insurance market is smaller too — and the insurers who built drone teams on the million-drone headline may find the premium pool thinner than they expected.

That caution is fair, and it does not change the conclusion. Even on the conservative count — roughly 390,000 commercial drones, 427,598 certificated remote pilots, and a market growing at about 9% a year — drone insurance is the fastest-growing segment of aviation cover, with the least mature underwriting and the most room for a specialist brand. The roofing contractor in Ohio did not need a lecture on risk. He needed a policy that named his aircraft, covered his liability, and cost less than the chimney. A million operators like him are the market. The name that serves them should say so in two words.
