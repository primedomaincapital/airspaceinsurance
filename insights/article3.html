The Fleet Is Getting Younger, Bigger, and Harder to Insure

Airbus and Boeing are delivering again, order books stretch a decade out, and every new airframe raises the insured value in the sky.

Prime Domain Capital Research · November 6, 2026 · 7 min read

On a January morning in 2026, Airbus announced it had delivered 793 commercial aircraft in 2025, up from 766 in 2024. Boeing, the same week, reported 600 deliveries, up from 348 the year before — its best annual total since 2018. The numbers were reported as a rivalry story, Airbus ahead, Boeing recovering. The insurance story is quieter and larger: every one of those 1,393 aircraft needs hull and liability cover before it carries a passenger, and every one of them is worth more than the aircraft it replaces.

The order books make the point sharper. Airbus closed 2025 with a backlog of 8,754 aircraft, and Boeing with more than 6,100 commercial airplanes — a combined backlog near 15,000 aircraft, roughly a decade of production at current rates. Boeing's own Commercial Market Outlook projects 43,600 new commercial aircraft deliveries through 2044, including 34,830 single-aisle jets, 7,815 widebodies, and 955 freighters. Each delivery is not just a sale. It is a new insured asset entering service, a new hull value to underwrite, and a new liability exposure attached to a larger, more expensive airframe.

The passengers are arriving too. The International Air Transport Association counted 4.779 billion segment passengers in 2024, with revenue passenger kilometres of 9,037 billion, and forecast 4.988 billion passengers in 2025 — growth of 5.8% after 10.6% in 2024. By late 2025, IATA was projecting about 4.98 billion passengers for the year, up from 4.77 billion in 2024, with airline profits of $39.5 billion. More passengers, on more aircraft, on more routes, means more liability exposure per flight hour, and more premium to price it.

General aviation tells a different version of the same story. The FAA's 2024 General Aviation and Part 135 Activity Survey counted 213,756 active aircraft, down slightly from 2023, but flying more than 29 million hours — up about 2% from 28.43 million in 2023. Piston aircraft remain the backbone: 138,960 active planes, flying 16.16 million hours. The fleet is old — nearly 30,000 aircraft are between 45 and 49 years old, and single-engine piston aircraft average more than 52 years old — and old aircraft are not cheap to insure. Parts are scarce, repair costs rise, and hull values for the newer aircraft entering the fleet are far higher than for the ones they replace. PricewaterhouseCoopers, in a study for the General Aviation Manufacturers Association, put general aviation's contribution to the United States economy at $339.2 billion in 2023, supporting 1.3 million jobs. Every one of those aircraft, and every one of those jobs, sits on top of an insurance policy.

The premium pool is growing with the fleet. Published estimates of the global aviation insurance market differ by scope — Reports and Data put it at $5.84 billion in 2025, rising to $9.67 billion by 2035 at a 5.2% annual rate; Emergen Research put it at $5.80 billion in 2025, rising to $9.70 billion by 2035 at 5.3%; Atlas Magazine put the 2025 figure at $4.695 billion, up from $3.759 billion in 2021. Hull insurance held the largest share, 42.5% in 2025 in the Emergen estimate, and war and terrorism cover was the fastest-growing line. The range of estimates is a warning in itself: this is a market measured by several different rulers. The direction, again, is not in dispute.

Underwriters are not simply riding the growth. They are re-pricing it. The Russia loss — more than 400 aircraft stranded, with a High Court judgment in June 2025 covering $4.5 billion of insured value — hardened war-risk rates and forced a re-examination of aggregate limits. Engine shortages, notably Pratt & Whitney supply problems cited by Airbus, are stretching delivery schedules and keeping older aircraft flying longer than planned, which changes the risk profile of the fleet in service. Certification of the remaining 737 MAX variants, and the 1,500-plus 737-10 aircraft in Boeing's backlog, will decide how quickly the newest single-aisle fleet reaches airlines. None of these are aviation-insurance stories on their own. All of them are, because each one changes what is in the sky and what it costs to cover.

The counterargument is that a backlog is not demand. A decade of orders can be cancelled, deferred, or repriced, and the delivery recovery of 2025 — Boeing up 72% from a strike- and scrutiny-depressed 2024 — flatters a comparison against a bad year. Airbus missed its original 820-delivery target for 2025, cutting it to 790 before delivering 793. The fleet is growing, but slowly, and the premium growth that follows it is hostage to production rates, engine supply, and certification timetables that insurers do not control. A domain buyer betting on fleet growth is betting on factories, not just on runways.

Fair enough — and still beside the point for this name. Whether the fleet grows by 4% or 7% a year, the insured value in the sky rises, the liability exposure rises, and the number of buyers who need to find an aviation insurer rises with them. The airlines, lessors, and operators who buy that cover will search for it, and the name they remember will be the one that states the risk and the product in two words. AirspaceInsurance.com does not need the backlog to be delivered on schedule. It needs the sky to stay busy, and insured. It is.
