Certified to Fly, Uninsurable to Operate: The Air Taxi's Insurance Gap

Joby is in the final stage of FAA certification. Archer is close behind. Neither can carry a paying passenger — because no underwriter has the data to price the risk.

Prime Domain Capital Research · November 9, 2026 · 7 min read

The city council approved the vertiport site in January. The aircraft earned its type certificate in February. The operator published a launch date in March. By April, only one question remained unanswered, and it had nothing to do with the FAA. No commercial air taxi operation launches without liability coverage. No liability coverage gets written without actuarial data. And for electric vertical takeoff and landing aircraft carrying passengers over American cities, that data does not exist. That is the insurance gap year — the period between type certification and the day underwriters have enough fleet data to write a commercially viable policy — and it is the part of the air taxi story nobody puts on the launch slide.

The certification race itself is real, and it is nearly over. Joby Aviation's S4 is in Stage 5, the final stage of FAA type certification — its August 5 letter put the fourth stage at 83 percent complete on Joby's side and 77 percent on the FAA's, with the fifth stage at 20 and 10 percent respectively — and its first FAA-conforming aircraft flew with Joby pilots on March 11, 2026, opening the door to “for credit” Type Inspection Authorization testing, the phase where government test pilots evaluate the aircraft themselves. Archer Aviation closed Phase 3 of its four-phase process in April, the first eVTOL company to do so, and is now in Phase 4 compliance work; the FAA accepted all 797 of the Midnight's proposed means of compliance back on March 2. The FAA only finalized its powered-lift pilot certification and operations framework in late 2024, as a Special Federal Aviation Regulation with a ten-year shelf life — temporary by design, because nobody, including the regulator, is confident enough to write permanent law yet. And the load-bearing fact, as of this autumn, is simple: no eVTOL air taxi holds an FAA type certificate. Not one aircraft in the sector is legally cleared for routine commercial passenger service in the United States.

WTW, the broker that has done the most public thinking about this, flagged four problems in April 2026 that keep aviation underwriters up at night. The first is lithium-ion battery fire risk in dense urban environments. The second is the absence of loss data — there is no claims history for a vehicle class that has never operated commercially. The third is shared-airspace liability: an air taxi over a city shares the sky with helicopters, drones, and general aviation, and the liability for a mid-air event has no precedent to price against. The fourth is autonomous-pilot ambiguity, as the sector moves toward operations where the question of who — or what — is the pilot has no settled legal answer. Unknown battery fire risk, absent loss data, shared-airspace liability, and autonomous-pilot ambiguity: four problems, none of them solvable by building a better aircraft.

The federal government's answer is data, collected the slow way. The FAA's Enhanced Integrated Pilot Program, launched in March 2026 across 26 states, will run a three-year operational data collection period that generates the first statistically meaningful dataset for eVTOL operations in urban environments. Eight projects were named in March, out of more than 30 proposals, under an executive order from June 2025. Joby began eIPP flights in Texas on September 10; Archer is targeting early operations in Florida, Texas, and New York in the second half of 2026. Florida's own program, SunTrax Air, is building two vertiports and what the state bills as the nation's first dedicated advanced air mobility aerial test track on a 775-acre campus between Tampa and Orlando, with eVTOL testing promised by the end of the year. The eIPP is almost never framed as an insurance infrastructure project. It should be. Three years of operational data is the actuarial runway the industry needs, and it is the only one on offer.

The money, meanwhile, is moving faster than the regulation. The eVTOL and advanced air mobility market is forecast to expand from $17.23 billion in 2026 to $128.22 billion by 2035, a compound annual growth rate of 24.98 percent, with passenger air mobility the largest segment at 34.85 percent. Joby raised a net $1.2 billion in February to survive the cash burn, is partnered with Toyota on automotive-style manufacturing and Virgin Atlantic on UK integration, and has targeted a commercial launch with Delta Air Lines before the end of 2026. Archer reported about $1.7 to $1.8 billion in liquidity. In July, Dubai's General Civil Aviation Authority granted operational certification to Skyports' VDX vertiport — the first purpose-built commercial vertiport anywhere to clear a regulator — a four-storey building with two landing areas, rapid charging, and a stated capacity of up to 170,000 passengers a year, with Joby holding a six-year exclusive for the Dubai market. A certified place to land is not a service: no commercial passenger flights operate from it yet. And the one place where passengers have actually flown is China, where the Civil Aviation Administration type-certified EHang's autonomous EH216-S in October 2023, the first passenger eVTOL type certificate anywhere.

Here is the counterargument, and the underwriters will make it themselves. The insurance gap is not a market failure. It is the market working. A risk with no loss history, no operating history, and no legal precedent for its failure modes should be expensive to insure, or uninsurable, until the history exists — and the history is being created right now, in Texas, in Florida, and in Dubai, by companies with billions of dollars of incentive to create it safely. The eIPP's three years will pass. The first policies will be written on demonstration data, then on limited operations, then on scale. Every new aviation segment in history — the jet, the helicopter, the drone — went through the same sequence, and the sequence ended with insurance. The gap year is not a verdict. It is a phase.

The flaw in that reassurance is the cash clock. These are pre-revenue companies burning capital against a regulatory timetable, and running out before certification is the field's characteristic failure mode. Joby holds roughly $810 million in cash plus Toyota's $894 million commitment; Archer's liquidity is the reason it is still in the race. A three-year data collection period is a long time to burn cash without revenue, and the companies' launch targets — Joby with Delta before the end of 2026, Archer in three states in the second half of 2026 — assume commercial operations follow certification quickly. None of those timelines build in an actuarial runway. If the first type certificate arrives and the first commercially viable policy does not, the launch date slips by the length of the gap, and the gap is measured in underwriting quarters, not press releases.

The implication for anyone pricing this risk is to watch the underwriters, not the unveilings. The type certificate is the headline; the first admitted liability policy for a piloted eVTOL over a city is the event. The eIPP data will tell the market what the risk actually costs, and until it does, the most honest price for air taxi liability is the one no underwriter will quote. The aircraft are nearly certified. The operations are nearly ready. The insurance — the piece that turns a demonstration into a business — is the part that has not been built yet, and it will be built on data that takes three years to collect. The sky is open. The policy is not.
